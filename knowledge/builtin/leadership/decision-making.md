# Executive decision-making

## Match the process to the decision
Most decisions are reversible and should be made quickly by the people closest to the work. A small number are hard to reverse, expensive or strategically defining; these deserve a deliberate process, more data and senior involvement. Treating every decision as irreversible slows the organization; treating irreversible decisions casually creates costly mistakes.

## Clarify decision rights
Ambiguity about who decides causes delays and rework. Decision-rights frameworks make roles explicit. In a RAPID-style model, people Recommend, Agree (hold a veto, used sparingly), Perform, provide Input, and one person Decides. In a DACI-style model there is a Driver, an Approver, Contributors and those Informed. Whatever the framework, there should be exactly one accountable decision-maker.

## Frame the decision well
State the decision as a choice between real alternatives, not a yes or no on one proposal. Write down the objective, constraints, options, key assumptions and the criteria for choosing. Ask what you would need to believe for each option to be the best one, then test the most uncertain beliefs.

## Use data without waiting for certainty
Decide when additional information is unlikely to change the choice or when the cost of delay exceeds the value of more data. Many experienced leaders decide with roughly 70 percent of the information they would ideally want, then correct course quickly.

## Reduce bias
Common biases include confirmation bias, anchoring, overconfidence, sunk-cost reasoning and group-think. Useful counter-measures are a pre-mortem (imagine the decision failed and explain why), assigning someone to argue the opposing case, collecting independent views before discussion, and comparing to base rates from similar situations.

## Disagree and commit
Once a decision is made, the team should execute it fully, even those who argued against it. Record the rationale and the signals that would trigger a review, so the decision can be revisited on evidence rather than relitigated on opinion.
