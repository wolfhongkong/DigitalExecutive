# The first 90 days in a new executive role

## Why the first 90 days matter
A new executive is judged quickly. Early decisions set expectations about pace, judgment and values, and early credibility makes later change easier. The goal of the first 90 days is not to fix everything but to understand the situation, secure a few visible wins and earn the right to make larger changes.

## Diagnose the situation first
Different situations need different strategies. A start-up needs structure and focus, a turnaround needs fast and decisive cuts, an accelerated-growth business needs scalable processes, a realignment needs the team to accept that change is necessary, and a sustaining success needs the leader to avoid breaking what works. Misdiagnosing the situation is a common cause of early failure.

## Learn systematically
Plan the learning instead of absorbing whatever arrives. Review financials, strategy documents, customer data and past board materials. Hold structured one-to-one conversations with direct reports, peers, key customers and front-line staff, asking the same questions so answers can be compared: What is working? What should change? What would you do in my position? What should I not touch?

## Agree on expectations with your manager or board
Align explicitly on the situation diagnosis, the expected results and timeline, the resources available, and how progress will be reported. Ambiguous expectations are a frequent reason why capable executives are seen as underperforming.

## Secure early wins
Pick two or three problems that matter to stakeholders, can be solved within a few months, and demonstrate the behaviors you want to build. Avoid early wins that rely only on your personal heroics; they should strengthen the team.

## Assess and shape the team
Evaluate each direct report on competence, judgment, energy, focus and trust. Decide who to keep, develop, move or replace, but avoid sweeping changes before the diagnosis is complete unless performance is clearly unacceptable.

## Common pitfalls
Arriving with a ready-made answer, trying to do too much, isolating yourself from informal networks, and repeating what worked in a previous company without checking whether the context is the same.
