# Process design and continuous improvement

## Why process matters
As organizations grow, informal coordination breaks down. Well-designed processes make work repeatable, measurable and improvable, and free leaders from constant firefighting. Process should serve outcomes; adding steps without a clear purpose creates bureaucracy.

## Mapping the current process
Start by mapping how work actually flows today, not how it is documented. Identify the trigger, steps, handoffs, decision points, systems and outputs. Value stream mapping distinguishes value-adding steps from waiting and rework and often reveals that most elapsed time is spent waiting.

## Common sources of waste
Lean thinking identifies typical wastes: overproduction, waiting, unnecessary transport or handoffs, over-processing, excess inventory or work in progress, unnecessary motion, and defects. In knowledge work, the biggest wastes are often waiting for approvals, context switching and rework caused by unclear requirements.

## Improvement methods
The plan-do-check-act cycle tests changes on a small scale before standardizing them. Root-cause analysis techniques such as the five whys and fishbone diagrams help fix causes rather than symptoms. Six Sigma's define-measure-analyze-improve-control approach suits problems where variation and defects can be measured precisely.

## Measuring processes
Track throughput, cycle time, first-pass yield or error rate, cost per transaction and customer satisfaction. Little's Law links work in progress, throughput and cycle time: reducing work in progress shortens cycle time without working harder.

## Standardize and sustain
Document the improved process in simple, visual form, assign a process owner, train people and monitor key metrics. Encourage front-line employees to suggest improvements; they see problems first. Automate only after the process is stable, because automating a poor process makes errors faster.
