# Compensation and leveling

## Compensation philosophy
A compensation philosophy states how the company pays relative to the market (for example at the median or the 75th percentile), the mix between fixed pay, variable pay and equity, and how pay is differentiated by performance. Writing it down makes decisions consistent and explainable.

## Job architecture and levels
A leveling framework defines career levels for each job family, with expectations for scope, impact, complexity and autonomy at each level. Many companies use parallel tracks for individual contributors and managers so that experts can progress without managing people. Levels create a shared language for hiring, promotion and pay.

## Salary bands
Each level has a salary band, typically with a midpoint based on market data and a range of about 20 to 30 percent on either side for professional roles. Market data comes from salary surveys and benchmarking providers matched by role, level, location and company size. Review bands at least annually.

## Variable pay and equity
Bonuses and sales commissions reward short-term results; equity aligns employees with long-term value. Equity grants are commonly sized by level and vest over several years with a one-year cliff. Explain equity clearly, including dilution and how value is realized, because employees often misunderstand it.

## Pay equity
Analyze pay regularly for unexplained differences by gender and other protected characteristics after controlling for level, role, location and performance. Correct unexplained gaps. Pay transparency requirements are expanding in many jurisdictions, including salary ranges in job postings and pay reporting.

## Making pay decisions
Run annual or semi-annual compensation reviews with a budget, guidelines that link increases to performance and position in band, and calibration across managers. Avoid ad hoc counteroffers, which create inequities and rarely retain people for long.
