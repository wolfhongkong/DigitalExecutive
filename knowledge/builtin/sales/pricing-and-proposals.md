# Pricing conversations and proposals

## Value-based pricing
Price is ultimately judged against the value a customer expects to receive and against their alternatives. Quantify the value with the customer during discovery, for example cost savings, revenue gains or risk reduction, so price can be discussed as a fraction of that value rather than as a cost.

## Packaging
Good-better-best tiers let customers self-select and anchor perception of value. Choose a value metric that grows with the customer's benefit, such as seats, usage or transactions. Keep packaging simple enough that buyers understand it without a long explanation.

## Handling price objections
Treat a price objection as a request for more information. Clarify whether the concern is budget, perceived value, comparison with a competitor or a negotiation tactic. Restate the agreed value and business case. Trade, do not give: if you reduce price, ask for something in return, such as a longer term, larger commitment, upfront payment, a case study or a faster signature.

## Discount discipline
Define discount guidelines and approval levels. Unmanaged discounting erodes margins and trains customers to wait for end-of-quarter deals. Track discount levels by representative and segment, and review deals where discounting did not shorten the cycle or increase win rates.

## Writing proposals
A strong proposal is about the customer, not the seller. It summarizes the customer's situation and goals in their words, the proposed solution and how it addresses each goal, the expected outcomes and how they will be measured, the implementation plan, the commercial terms and clear next steps. Keep the executive summary to one page for senior decision-makers.

## Mutual action plans
For complex deals, agree a mutual action plan with the buyer listing the steps, owners and dates to a decision and go-live, including legal, security and procurement reviews. It reveals the true decision process and reduces surprises.
