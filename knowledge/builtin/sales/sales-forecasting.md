# Sales forecasting

## Purpose
A sales forecast estimates revenue that will close in a period. It drives hiring, spending and investor communication, so accuracy and consistency matter more than optimism.

## Forecast categories
Many teams classify opportunities as commit (high confidence it will close in the period), best case (could close with favorable developments), pipeline (unlikely this period) and closed. Categories should be defined by objective criteria, such as verbal agreement from the economic buyer and a signed-off procurement timeline for commit.

## Methods
Bottom-up forecasts aggregate representative judgments on individual deals. Weighted pipeline forecasts multiply each deal's value by a stage-based probability derived from historical conversion rates. Historical or trend forecasts extrapolate run rates, useful for high-volume transactional sales. Comparing several methods highlights over- or under-confidence.

## Improving accuracy
Inspect deals rather than accepting summary numbers: what has the customer done, not what has the seller done. Track forecast accuracy over time for each manager. Remove deals with slipped close dates or no recent buyer engagement. Account for seasonality and end-of-quarter patterns.

## Recurring revenue businesses
For subscription businesses, forecast new bookings, expansion, contraction and churn separately. Net revenue retention, the recurring revenue from an existing cohort after a year including expansion and churn, is a key driver; above 100 percent means the existing base grows on its own.

## Communicating the forecast
Present a single number with a range and the main risks and upsides, and explain changes since the last forecast. Consistently landing within a narrow range builds credibility with leadership and investors.
